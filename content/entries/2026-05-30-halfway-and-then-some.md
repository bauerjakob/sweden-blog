I've been trying to write this one for a week, which is usually a sign the
thing is worth writing. So: the light. I have to talk about the light, because
it has quietly become the main character of the whole semester, and if I don't
put it down now I'll forget exactly how strange it felt.

When I arrived in January the sun did about six and a half hours of work a day
and clearly resented every minute of it. It came up grudgingly around nine,
skimmed low across the rooftops without ever really committing, and was gone
again by half three. For the first month I genuinely thought something was
wrong with me. I was tired in a way that sleep didn't fix. Everyone here just
nodded and said *vintermörker* — winter darkness — like it was weather, which
I suppose it is.

## What the dark teaches you

Here's the thing nobody at home understood when I complained down the phone:
the dark isn't only hard. It does something to how a room feels. You light
candles at four in the afternoon not because it's romantic but because it's
February and you have to, and after a while it stops being a coping mechanism
and becomes just… how the evening is. Small, warm, deliberate. You learn to
make the inside of a day.

> The Swedes have a whole practice around this and only an ocean of coffee to
> show for it. I have come to believe the coffee is load-bearing.

I got good at small rituals I'd have found precious in September:

- the walk to the same bakery, even when it was minus twelve;
- the exact tram window seat on the 6, on the left, facing backwards;
- coffee at a fixed time with the same three people, no phones, no agenda.

None of that is content. None of it would survive being posted anywhere. But
it's the actual texture of the time I spent here, and I want the version of me
reading this in ten years to know that the semester wasn't the trips and the
photos. It was mostly this. A warm room and a fixed tram seat and learning to
sit inside a long pause without panicking.

## And then the light comes back

I wasn't ready for how *fast* it turns. Some week in late March the maths
flips and suddenly the days are gaining light quicker than you can keep track
of — five, six minutes a day, which doesn't sound like much until you realise
it compounds. By April I was walking home from the library in daylight. By May
I was walking home from *dinner* in daylight. Right now, tonight, as I write
this, it is nearly half past nine and the sky over the lake is doing the thing
you see in the photo up there — not sunset, just a long, patient, gold-grey
refusal to get dark at all.

The locals go a little feral about it, and I understand completely. The moment
the sun is out, the entire city is outside. Every scrap of grass has a person
on it. People eat lunch on the steps of buildings with their faces tipped back
like plants. There's an unhurried, almost greedy quality to how they use a
warm afternoon, and now I know it's because they've counted. They know exactly
how much of this there is and exactly what November costs.

## The part I didn't expect

I came here for a semester of a degree and I'll leave with the credits, and if
you'd asked me in January what I'd remember I'd have said the courses, the
travel, some good nights out. All of which happened. But the thing that's
actually changed is smaller and harder to explain to people back home without
sounding like I've joined something.

It's this: I used to treat empty time as a problem to solve. A gap in the
calendar was something to fill, ideally with something worth mentioning. Here,
where for months there was simply less day to go around, I stopped doing that.
I learned that a slow afternoon isn't wasted. That a walk with no destination
counts. That you can sit across a table from someone in comfortable silence and
that this is, in fact, the good part — not the awkward run-up to the good part.

I don't know how much of that survives the flight home. Probably less than I'd
like. Habits are local; you leave a lot of yourself at the airport. But I
wanted it written down while it's still true, in a place my family can read it,
so that at least one honest version of this exists that isn't a photo of a bun.

There's a month left. The light will keep stretching until it barely bothers
setting at all, and then, imperceptibly, it'll start the long walk back toward
the dark I arrived in — except I won't be here to see that part. I'll be home,
where the days are sensible and the same length all year, trying to explain to
people why I keep lighting candles in the afternoon.

Anyway. That's the light. Thanks for following along. There are still buns to
document.
